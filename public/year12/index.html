Vector Geometry (1)

Question 1

Use vector geometry to prove that $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$.

SolutionShow

We prove the result geometrically by showing that both sides represent the same directed segment from a common starting point.

Let $O$ be an origin in the plane (or in space). Represent the vectors by directed segments:

$\overrightarrow{OA} = \mathbf{u}$, then $\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{v}$, then $\overrightarrow{BC} = \mathbf{w}$.

By the triangle law of vector addition,

$$\overrightarrow{OB} = \overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}$$

$$\overrightarrow{OC} = \overrightarrow{OB} + \overrightarrow{BC} = (\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w}$$

On the other hand, the single directed segment from $A$ to $C$ is $\overrightarrow{AC} = \overrightarrow{AB} + \overrightarrow{BC} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w}$.

Applying the triangle law again from $O$ to $C$ via $A$,

$$\overrightarrow{OC} = \overrightarrow{OA} + \overrightarrow{AC} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$$

Therefore both $(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w}$ and $\mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$ equal the same vector $\overrightarrow{OC}$.

$$(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) + \mathbf{w} = \mathbf{u} + (\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{w})$$

Hence vector addition is associative.

The same argument works in three dimensions: the construction uses only the triangle law along a broken line of directed segments, which is valid in space.

Question 2

(a) Use a diagram to show that the distances from the vector $\mathbf{u} = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$ to the $x$-$y$, $y$-$z$ and $x$-$z$ planes are $z_0$, $x_0$ and $y_0$ respectively.

(b) If $\alpha$, $\beta$, $\gamma$ are the angles that the vector $\mathbf{u} = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$ makes with the positive $x$, $y$ and $z$-axes respectively, prove that $\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1$.

Solution (a)Show

Identify the tip of $\mathbf{u}$ with the point $P(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ in $xyz$-space (taking the common initial point of position vectors as the origin $O$).

The coordinate planes and the perpendicular distances from $P$ to them are:

• $x$-$y$ plane: $z = 0$. The foot of the perpendicular from $P$ is $(x_0,y_0,0)$, so the distance is $|z_0|$. (In the usual first-octant diagram one writes this as $z_0$.)

• $y$-$z$ plane: $x = 0$. The foot is $(0,y_0,z_0)$, so the distance is $|x_0|$ (shown as $x_0$).

• $x$-$z$ plane: $y = 0$. The foot is $(x_0,0,z_0)$, so the distance is $|y_0|$ (shown as $y_0$).

Thus the distances from $\mathbf{u} = (x_0,y_0,z_0)$ to the $x$-$y$, $y$-$z$ and $x$-$z$ planes are $z_0$, $x_0$ and $y_0$ respectively.

Solution (b)Show

Let $|\mathbf{u}| = r = \sqrt{x_0^2 + y_0^2 + z_0^2}$ (assume $\mathbf{u} \neq \mathbf{0}$).

By definition of the direction angles,

$$\cos\alpha = \dfrac{x_0}{r},\qquad \cos\beta = \dfrac{y_0}{r},\qquad \cos\gamma = \dfrac{z_0}{r}$$

Therefore

$$\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = \dfrac{x_0^2}{r^2} + \dfrac{y_0^2}{r^2} + \dfrac{z_0^2}{r^2} = \dfrac{x_0^2 + y_0^2 + z_0^2}{r^2} = \dfrac{r^2}{r^2} = 1$$

Hence $\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1$.

Question 1 (Level 3)

The points $A$, $B$, $C$ and $D$ form a parallelogram, where $A = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\1\end{pmatrix}$, $B = \begin{pmatrix}-3\\5\\2\end{pmatrix}$ and $C = \begin{pmatrix}1\\3\\4\end{pmatrix}$. Find the possible position vectors of $D$.

SolutionShow

The order of the vertices is not specified, so any one of $A$, $B$ or $C$ may be opposite $D$. Equivalently, the midpoint of one of the segments $AB$, $AC$ or $BC$ is also the midpoint of the other diagonal.

Case 1: $AB$ and $CD$ are diagonals (so $C$ and $D$ complete with midpoint of $AB$).

$$\dfrac{A+B}{2} = \dfrac{C+D}{2} \Rightarrow D = A + B - C$$

$$D = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\1\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}-3\\5\\2\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}1\\3\\4\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-5\\4\\-1\end{pmatrix}$$

Case 2: $AC$ and $BD$ are diagonals.

$$\dfrac{A+C}{2} = \dfrac{B+D}{2} \Rightarrow D = A + C - B$$

$$D = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\1\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}1\\3\\4\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}-3\\5\\2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}3\\0\\3\end{pmatrix}$$

Case 3: $BC$ and $AD$ are diagonals.

$$\dfrac{B+C}{2} = \dfrac{A+D}{2} \Rightarrow D = B + C - A$$

$$D = \begin{pmatrix}-3\\5\\2\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix}1\\3\\4\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}-1\\2\\1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-1\\6\\5\end{pmatrix}$$

Answer: the possible position vectors of $D$ are $\begin{pmatrix}-5\\4\\-1\end{pmatrix}$, $\begin{pmatrix}3\\0\\3\end{pmatrix}$ and $\begin{pmatrix}-1\\6\\5\end{pmatrix}$.

Question 2 (Level 3)

In the diagram, $\overrightarrow{OA} = 3\mathbf{a}$, $\overrightarrow{OB} = 6\mathbf{b}$ and $\overrightarrow{YB} = 5\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$.

(a) Express $\overrightarrow{AB}$ and $\overrightarrow{OY}$ in terms of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$.

(b) Find the ratio in which $X$ divides $AB$ and $OY$.

Solution (a)Show

Take $O$ as the origin, so the position vectors are $\mathbf{A} = 3\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{B} = 6\mathbf{b}$.

Since $\overrightarrow{YB} = \mathbf{B} - \mathbf{Y} = 5\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}$,

$$\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{B} - (5\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}) = 6\mathbf{b} - 5\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{b} = -5\mathbf{a} + 7\mathbf{b}$$

$$\overrightarrow{AB} = \mathbf{B} - \mathbf{A} = 6\mathbf{b} - 3\mathbf{a} = -3\mathbf{a} + 6\mathbf{b}$$

$$\overrightarrow{OY} = \mathbf{Y} = -5\mathbf{a} + 7\mathbf{b}$$

Answer: $\overrightarrow{AB} = -3\mathbf{a} + 6\mathbf{b}$, $\overrightarrow{OY} = -5\mathbf{a} + 7\mathbf{b}$.

Solution (b)Show

Let $X$ divide $AB$ in the ratio $\lambda : 1$, so $\overrightarrow{AX}:\overrightarrow{XB} = \lambda:1$. Then

$$\mathbf{X} = \dfrac{\mathbf{A} + \lambda\mathbf{B}}{\lambda + 1} = \dfrac{3\mathbf{a} + 6\lambda\mathbf{b}}{\lambda + 1}$$

Let $X$ also divide $OY$ in the ratio $\mu : 1$, so $\overrightarrow{OX}:\overrightarrow{XY} = \mu:1$. Then

$$\mathbf{X} = \dfrac{\mu\mathbf{Y}}{\mu + 1} = \dfrac{\mu(-5\mathbf{a} + 7\mathbf{b})}{\mu + 1}$$

Equating coefficients of $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ (assuming $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$ independent),

$$\dfrac{3}{\lambda + 1} = \dfrac{-5\mu}{\mu + 1}, \qquad \dfrac{6\lambda}{\lambda + 1} = \dfrac{7\mu}{\mu + 1}$$

From the second equation,

$$6\lambda(\mu + 1) = 7\mu(\lambda + 1) \Rightarrow 6\lambda - 7\mu = \lambda\mu$$

$$\lambda = \dfrac{7\mu}{6 - \mu}$$

Substitute into the first equation:

$$3(\mu + 1) = -5\mu(\lambda + 1)$$

$$3 + 8\mu = -5\lambda\mu$$

$$3 + 8\mu = -5\mu\cdot\dfrac{7\mu}{6 - \mu} = \dfrac{-35\mu^2}{6 - \mu}$$

$$(3 + 8\mu)(6 - \mu) = -35\mu^2$$

$$18 + 48\mu - 3\mu - 8\mu^2 = -35\mu^2$$

$$27\mu^2 + 45\mu + 18 = 0$$

$$3\mu^2 + 5\mu + 2 = 0 \Rightarrow (3\mu + 2)(\mu + 1) = 0$$

So $\mu = -1$ (invalid, since $\mu + 1 = 0$) or $\mu = -\dfrac{2}{3}$.

$$\lambda = \dfrac{7(-\tfrac{2}{3})}{6 - (-\tfrac{2}{3})} = \dfrac{-\tfrac{14}{3}}{\tfrac{20}{3}} = -\dfrac{7}{10}$$

Hence $X$ divides $AB$ in the ratio $\overrightarrow{AX}:\overrightarrow{XB} = -7:10$ (i.e. externally in the ratio $7:10$), and divides $OY$ in the ratio $\overrightarrow{OX}:\overrightarrow{XY} = -2:3$ (externally in the ratio $2:3$).

Answer: $X$ divides $AB$ in the ratio $7:10$ externally, and divides $OY$ in the ratio $2:3$ externally.

Question 2 (Projections)

Given nonzero vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$, prove the following identities using a geometric argument:

(i) $\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}(-\mathbf{v}) = -\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{v})$

Solution (i)Show

Recall that $\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{v})$ is the vector along the line through $\mathbf{u}$ obtained by dropping a perpendicular from the tip of $\mathbf{v}$ onto that line.

Replace $\mathbf{v}$ by $-\mathbf{v}$. The tip of $-\mathbf{v}$ is the point diametrically opposite the tip of $\mathbf{v}$ through the common initial point. The perpendicular from the tip of $-\mathbf{v}$ to the line of $\mathbf{u}$ therefore meets that line at the point opposite $\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{v})$ with respect to the origin.

Hence the projected vector is exactly the negative of $\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{v})$:

$$\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}(-\mathbf{v}) = -\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{v})$$

(Algebraically the same conclusion follows from $\mathrm{proj}_{\mathbf{u}}(\mathbf{v})=\dfrac{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u}}{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{u}}\mathbf{u}$, since $(-\mathbf{v})\cdot\mathbf{u}=-(\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{u})$, but the question asks for a geometric argument.)

Question 3

Use vector methods to prove Pythagoras' theorem.

SolutionShow

Let $\triangle ABC$ be right-angled at $C$. Write $\mathbf{a}=\overrightarrow{CA}$ and $\mathbf{b}=\overrightarrow{CB}$, and let $\mathbf{c}=\overrightarrow{AB}$.

Then $\mathbf{c}=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}$, and since $\angle C=90^\circ$ the sides $CA$ and $CB$ are perpendicular, so

$$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=0$$

Now expand $|\mathbf{c}|^2=\mathbf{c}\cdot\mathbf{c}$:

$$|\mathbf{c}|^2 = (\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a})\cdot(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}) = |\mathbf{b}|^2 - 2\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} + |\mathbf{a}|^2$$

Using $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=0$,

$$|\mathbf{c}|^2 = |\mathbf{a}|^2 + |\mathbf{b}|^2$$

But $|\mathbf{a}|=CA$, $|\mathbf{b}|=CB$ and $|\mathbf{c}|=AB$, so

$$AB^2 = CA^2 + CB^2$$

Hence Pythagoras' theorem holds.

(Equivalently: if $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are orthogonal, then $|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}|^2=|\mathbf{u}|^2+|\mathbf{v}|^2$, since the cross term $2\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ vanishes.)

Source: Year 12 (A1) Mathematics Extension 1 — Optional Challenging Questions (Level 3).

Exercise 1 — Question 1

Using implicit differentiation, find the derivative of $y = \sqrt{\dfrac{x-1}{x+1}}$ in terms of $x$ only.

SolutionShow

For $x>1$ we have $y>0$. Take natural logarithms of both sides:

$$\ln y = \dfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\ln(x-1)-\ln(x+1)\bigr)$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$:

$$\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{x-1}-\dfrac{1}{x+1}\right) = \dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{2}{(x-1)(x+1)} = \dfrac{1}{x^2-1}$$

$$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{y}{x^2-1} = \dfrac{1}{x^2-1}\sqrt{\dfrac{x-1}{x+1}}$$

Equivalently,

$$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{1}{(x+1)\sqrt{x^2-1}}$$

Answer: $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{x^2-1}\sqrt{\dfrac{x-1}{x+1}}$.

Alternative (squaring both sides)Show

Square both sides (valid for $x>1$, where $y>0$):

$$y^2 = \dfrac{x-1}{x+1}$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$ (quotient rule on the right, chain rule on the left):

$$2y\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{(1)(x+1)-(x-1)(1)}{(x+1)^2} = \dfrac{2}{(x+1)^2}$$

$$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{1}{y(x+1)^2}$$

Rewrite using $y^2=\dfrac{x-1}{x+1}$:

$$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{y}{y^2(x+1)^2} = \dfrac{y}{\left(\dfrac{x-1}{x+1}\right)(x+1)^2} = \dfrac{y}{x^2-1} = \dfrac{1}{x^2-1}\sqrt{\dfrac{x-1}{x+1}}$$

Same answer as above.

Exercise 1 — Question 2

By taking logarithms of both sides, differentiate $y=x^x$.

SolutionShow

For $x>0$, write $y=x^x=e^{x\ln x}$, or take logs:

$$\ln y = x\ln x$$

$$\dfrac{1}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \ln x + x\cdot\dfrac{1}{x} = \ln x + 1$$

$$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = x^x(\ln x + 1)$$

Answer: $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=x^x(\ln x+1)$.

Exercise 1 — Question 3

The variables $x$ and $y$ are positive and related by $x^a y^b = (x+y)^{a+b}$ where $a$ and $b$ are positive constants. By taking logarithms of both sides, show that $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{y}{x}$, provided that $bx\neq ay$.

SolutionShow

$$a\ln x + b\ln y = (a+b)\ln(x+y)$$

Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$:

$$\dfrac{a}{x} + \dfrac{b}{y}\dfrac{dy}{dx} = (a+b)\cdot\dfrac{1}{x+y}\left(1+\dfrac{dy}{dx}\right)$$

Multiply through by $xy(x+y)$ to clear denominators, or rearrange for $y'$:

$$\dfrac{b}{y}y' - \dfrac{a+b}{x+y}y' = \dfrac{a+b}{x+y} - \dfrac{a}{x}$$

$$y'\left(\dfrac{b}{y}-\dfrac{a+b}{x+y}\right) = \dfrac{a(x+y)-(a+b)y}{x(x+y)} = \dfrac{ax-by}{x(x+y)}$$

$$y'\cdot\dfrac{b(x+y)-y(a+b)}{y(x+y)} = \dfrac{ax-by}{x(x+y)}$$

$$y'\cdot\dfrac{bx-ay}{y(x+y)} = \dfrac{ax-by}{x(x+y)} = \dfrac{-(bx-ay)}{x(x+y)}$$

Since $bx\neq ay$, cancel $bx-ay$:

$$y'\cdot\dfrac{1}{y} = -\dfrac{1}{x}\cdot(-1) \quad\text{(after careful sign check)}$$

More cleanly: from the previous line,

$$y' = \dfrac{ax-by}{x(x+y)}\cdot\dfrac{y(x+y)}{bx-ay} = \dfrac{(ax-by)y}{x(bx-ay)} = \dfrac{y}{x}$$

since $ax-by=-(bx-ay)$.

Hence $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{y}{x}$.

Exercise 2 — Question 1

(a) Show that the tangent from the origin to the curve $y=\ln x$ has gradient $\dfrac{1}{e}$.

(b) Hence find the set of values of the real number $k$ for which the equation $\ln x = kx$ has two distinct roots.

Solution (a)Show

Let the point of tangency be $(t,\ln t)$ with $t>0$. Then $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{x}$, so the gradient at $x=t$ is $\dfrac{1}{t}$.

The tangent line is

$$Y-\ln t = \dfrac{1}{t}(X-t)$$

It passes through the origin $(0,0)$:

$$0-\ln t = \dfrac{1}{t}(0-t) = -1 \Rightarrow \ln t = 1 \Rightarrow t=e$$

Therefore the gradient is $\dfrac{1}{t}=\dfrac{1}{e}$.

Solution (b)Show

The equation $\ln x=kx$ ($x>0$) is equivalent to $k=\dfrac{\ln x}{x}$ whenever we seek intersections of $y=\ln x$ and the line $y=kx$.

Consider $g(x)=\dfrac{\ln x}{x}$ for $x>0$.

$$g'(x)=\dfrac{1-\ln x}{x^2}$$

So $g'(x)=0$ at $x=e$, and $g$ has a maximum there with $g(e)=\dfrac{1}{e}$.

Also $g(x)\to 0$ as $x\to\infty$, $g(x)\to-\infty$ as $x\to 0^+$, and $g(x)\lt 0$ on $(0,1)$.

Hence:

• if $k=\dfrac{1}{e}$: exactly one positive root ($x=e$);

• if $0\lt k\lt\dfrac{1}{e}$: exactly two distinct positive roots;

• if $k\le 0$: at most one positive root.

Answer: two distinct roots when $0\lt k\lt\dfrac{1}{e}$.

Exercise 3 — Question 1

(a) Differentiate $y=x^x$ by taking logarithms of both sides. Using this, describe the behaviour of the curve $y=x^x$ near $x=0$ and show that it becomes vertical as $x\to 0^+$. You may use $\lim_{x\to 0^+} x\ln x=0$.

(b) Determine the nature of any stationary points and find the point(s) on the curve where the tangent has gradient $1$.

(c) Sketch the function.

Solution (a)Show

$$\ln y = x\ln x \Rightarrow \dfrac{y'}{y}=\ln x+1 \Rightarrow y'=x^x(\ln x+1)$$

As $x\to 0^+$, $x\ln x\to 0$, so $x^x=e^{x\ln x}\to e^0=1$. Thus the curve approaches height $1$.

The gradient $y'=x^x(\ln x+1)\to 1\cdot(-\infty)=-\infty$ as $x\to 0^+$, so the tangent becomes vertical.

Solution (b)Show

Stationary points: $y'=0\Rightarrow \ln x+1=0\Rightarrow x=e^{-1}$ (since $x^x>0$).

$$y\left(e^{-1}\right)=\left(e^{-1}\right)^{e^{-1}}=e^{-1/e}$$

For $0\lt x\lt e^{-1}$, $\ln x+1\lt 0$ so $y'\lt 0$; for $x>e^{-1}$, $y'>0$. Hence a local minimum at $\left(e^{-1},e^{-1/e}\right)$.

Where the gradient is $1$:

$$x^x(\ln x+1)=1$$

For $0\lt x\lt e^{-1}$, $\ln x+1\lt 0$, so $y'\lt 0\neq 1$.

For $x>e^{-1}$, $y'$ increases continuously from $0$ (at the minimum) to $\infty$.

At $x=1$: $1^1(\ln 1+1)=1$. Since $y'$ is continuous and strictly increases through the value $1$ only once on $(e^{-1},\infty)$, this is the unique solution.

Answer: local minimum at $\left(e^{-1},e^{-1/e}\right)$; tangent has gradient $1$ at $(1,1)$.

Solution (c)Show

Using (a) and (b), the sketch of $y=x^x$ for $x>0$ has these features:

• as $x\to 0^+$, $y\to 1$ with a vertical tangent;

• a local minimum at $\left(e^{-1},e^{-1/e}\right)$;

• passes through $(1,1)$, where the gradient is $1$;

• increasing for $x>e^{-1}$, with $y\to\infty$ as $x\to\infty$.

Exercise 3 — Question 2

In the diagram, the curve $y=P(x)$ is tangential to the $x$-axis at $x=a$ where $0\lt a\lt 1$. Explain, using calculus, whether or not the curve $y=(\ln x)\,P(x)$ will be tangential to the $x$-axis at $x=a$. Then, on the same set of axes, sketch the curve $y=(\ln x)\,P(x)$.

SolutionShow

Since $y=P(x)$ is tangential to the $x$-axis at $x=a$,

$$P(a)=0 \quad\text{and}\quad P'(a)=0$$

Let $f(x)=(\ln x)\,P(x)$ (defined for $x>0$). Then

$$f(a)=\ln a\cdot P(a)=0$$

$$f'(x)=\dfrac{1}{x}P(x)+\ln x\cdot P'(x)$$

$$f'(a)=\dfrac{1}{a}P(a)+\ln a\cdot P'(a)=0$$

So $f(a)=f'(a)=0$: yes, $y=(\ln x)\,P(x)$ is also tangential to the $x$-axis at $x=a$.

Sketch (same axes; $P$ shown lighter for reference):

• Domain $x>0$. Near $x=0^+$, $P(x)$ behaves like a multiple of $x$, and $x\ln x\to 0$, so $f(x)\to 0$.

• Touches the $x$-axis at $x=a$ (still tangential).

• For $0\lt x\lt 1$, $\ln x\lt 0$, so $f$ has the opposite sign to $P$; for $x>1$, $\ln x>0$, so $f$ has the same sign as $P$.

• At $x=1$, $f(1)=\ln 1\cdot P(1)=0$, so the sketch crosses the $x$-axis at $x=1$.

Calculus of Logarithmic Function (II)

Source: Year 12 (A1) Mathematics Extension 1 — Optional Challenging Questions (Level 3).

Exercise 1 — Question 1

(a) Show that $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[\dfrac{1}{2a}\ln\left|\dfrac{x-a}{x+a}\right|\right]=\dfrac{1}{x^2-a^2}$ for $a>0$. Hence find $\displaystyle\int\dfrac{1}{x^2-a^2}\,dx$.

(b) Hence, by using the substitution $x=u^2$ or otherwise, find $\displaystyle\int\dfrac{\sqrt{x}}{x-1}\,dx$.

Solution (a)Show

$$\dfrac{1}{2a}\ln\left|\dfrac{x-a}{x+a}\right|=\dfrac{1}{2a}\bigl(\ln|x-a|-\ln|x+a|\bigr)$$

Differentiate:

$$\dfrac{d}{dx}=\dfrac{1}{2a}\left(\dfrac{1}{x-a}-\dfrac{1}{x+a}\right)=\dfrac{1}{2a}\cdot\dfrac{(x+a)-(x-a)}{(x-a)(x+a)}=\dfrac{1}{2a}\cdot\dfrac{2a}{x^2-a^2}=\dfrac{1}{x^2-a^2}$$

Therefore, integrating both sides,

$$\int\dfrac{1}{x^2-a^2}\,dx=\dfrac{1}{2a}\ln\left|\dfrac{x-a}{x+a}\right|+C$$

Solution (b)Show

Put $x=u^2$ (so $u=\sqrt{x}$ for $x>0$), giving $dx=2u\,du$.

$$\int\dfrac{\sqrt{x}}{x-1}\,dx=\int\dfrac{u}{u^2-1}\cdot 2u\,du=\int\dfrac{2u^2}{u^2-1}\,du=\int\left(2+\dfrac{2}{u^2-1}\right)\,du$$

Using part (a) with $a=1$:

$$\int\dfrac{2}{u^2-1}\,du=2\cdot\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left|\dfrac{u-1}{u+1}\right|=\ln\left|\dfrac{u-1}{u+1}\right|$$

$$\int\dfrac{\sqrt{x}}{x-1}\,dx=2u+\ln\left|\dfrac{u-1}{u+1}\right|+C=2\sqrt{x}+\ln\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{x}-1}{\sqrt{x}+1}\right|+C$$

Answer: $2\sqrt{x}+\ln\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{x}-1}{\sqrt{x}+1}\right|+C$.

Exercise 2 — Question 1

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{1}^{27}\dfrac{3}{\bigl(x^{1/3}+1\bigr)\,x^{2/3}}\,dx$.

SolutionShow

Let $u=x^{1/3}$, so $x=u^3$ and $dx=3u^2\,du$.

When $x=1$, $u=1$; when $x=27$, $u=3$.

$$\int_{1}^{27}\dfrac{3}{(x^{1/3}+1)\,x^{2/3}}\,dx=\int_{1}^{3}\dfrac{3}{(u+1)\,u^2}\cdot 3u^2\,du=\int_{1}^{3}\dfrac{9}{u+1}\,du$$

$$=9\ln|u+1|\Big|_{1}^{3}=9\bigl(\ln 4-\ln 2\bigr)=9\ln 2$$

Answer: $9\ln 2$.

Exercise 2 — Question 2

(a) Using the substitution $u=\dfrac{1}{x}$, show that $\displaystyle\int_{1/2}^{1}\dfrac{\ln x}{1+x^2}\,dx=\int_{2}^{1}\dfrac{\ln u}{1+u^2}\,du$.

(b) Deduce the value of $\displaystyle\int_{1/2}^{2}\dfrac{\ln x}{1+x^2}\,dx$.

Solution (a)Show

Let $u=\dfrac{1}{x}$, so $x=\dfrac{1}{u}$ and $dx=-\dfrac{1}{u^2}\,du$.

When $x=\dfrac{1}{2}$, $u=2$; when $x=1$, $u=1$.

$$\int_{1/2}^{1}\dfrac{\ln x}{1+x^2}\,dx=\int_{2}^{1}\dfrac{\ln(1/u)}{1+1/u^2}\left(-\dfrac{1}{u^2}\right)\,du$$

$$=\int_{2}^{1}\dfrac{-\ln u}{(u^2+1)/u^2}\left(-\dfrac{1}{u^2}\right)\,du=\int_{2}^{1}\dfrac{\ln u}{1+u^2}\,du$$

Solution (b)Show

$$f'(x)=-\sin 2x-\sin\!\left(2x+\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\right)-\sin\!\left(2x-\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\right)$$

The three angles $2x$, $2x+\dfrac{4\pi}{3}$, $2x-\dfrac{4\pi}{3}$ are equally spaced by $\dfrac{4\pi}{3}$. Their sines sum to zero:

$$\sin\theta+\sin\!\left(\theta+\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\right)+\sin\!\left(\theta-\dfrac{4\pi}{3}\right)=2\sin\theta\cos\dfrac{4\pi}{3}+\sin\theta=\sin\theta\bigl(1+2\cdot(-\tfrac{1}{2})\bigr)=0$$

with $\theta=2x$. Hence $f'(x)=0$ for all $x$, so $f$ is constant.

$$f(0)=1+\cos^2\dfrac{2\pi}{3}+\cos^2\!\left(-\dfrac{2\pi}{3}\right)=1+\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^2+\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^2=1+\dfrac{1}{4}+\dfrac{1}{4}=\dfrac{3}{2}$$

Therefore $f(x)=\dfrac{3}{2}$ for all real $x$.

The curves $y=\cos x$ and $y=\tan x$ intersect at a point $P$ whose $x$-coordinate is $\alpha$.

(a) Show that the curves intersect at right angles at $P$.

Solution (a)Show

At $P$, $\cos\alpha=\tan\alpha$, so $\cos^2\alpha=\sin\alpha$ (and $\cos\alpha\neq 0$).

$$\dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos x)=-\sin x,\qquad \dfrac{d}{dx}(\tan x)=\sec^2 x$$

The product of the gradients at $x=\alpha$ is

$$(-\sin\alpha)\sec^2\alpha=-\dfrac{\sin\alpha}{\cos^2\alpha}=-1$$

using $\sin\alpha=\cos^2\alpha$. Hence the tangents are perpendicular, so the curves intersect at right angles at $P$.

Solution (b)Show

From $\sin\alpha=\cos^2\alpha$ and $\sin^2\alpha+\cos^2\alpha=1$,

$$\cos^4\alpha+\cos^2\alpha-1=0$$

Put $u=\cos^2\alpha=\dfrac{1}{\sec^2\alpha}$. Then $u^2+u-1=0$, so

$$u=\dfrac{-1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \sec^2\alpha=\dfrac{1}{u}=\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{5}-1}=\dfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}$$
